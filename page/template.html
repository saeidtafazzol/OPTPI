Optimization as Sampling: Path Integral Samplers

From the Boltzmann distribution to learned stochastic controls, trained with the forward KL (PICE) on a multi-basin problem
Saeid Tafazzol · October 2026 · code on GitHub

1Optimization as a sampling problem

1.1Local vs global search

Suppose that we are trying to minimize an energy function \(U(x)\) (as an example, the 2-D toy problem in Fig. 1). We have our friendly neighborhood gradient-based algorithms, which need an initial point, and most of them get stuck in the local minimum of the initial point's basin. Therefore, global search algorithms, such as the meta-heuristic Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) or Simulated Annealing (SA), can be employed in a hybrid manner: they find the globally optimal basins, and within those basins the gradient-based algorithms optimize locally.

  • Goal: minimize an energy \(U(x)\) (e.g., the 2-D toy in Fig. 1).
  • Gradient-based methods need an initial point and get stuck in its basin.
  • Hybrid: a global search (PSO, SA) finds the globally optimal basins; gradient-based methods optimize locally within them.
Fig. 1. The toy energy \(U(x)\): two equally deep, narrow basins A and B (\(U = 0\)), far from each other, and four shallower, wider basins C–F, separated by a plateau at \(U \approx 2.5\). E sits next to the origin. Drag to rotate.

1.2The Boltzmann view

Another way to look at optimization is sampling: finding the minimum of \(U(x)\) is exactly the same as sampling from the Boltzmann distribution

$$P(x) = \lim_{T \downarrow 0} \frac{e^{-U(x)/T}}{Z(T)}, \qquad Z(T) = \int e^{-U(x)/T}\,dx. \tag{1}$$

Note that if there are multiple minimizers with equal \(U(x)\), this sampler will pick all of them: uniformly when they have equal minima and curvature, and in general with probabilities \(\propto 1/\sqrt{\det \nabla^2 U}\) at each minimizer.

  • Multiple minimizers with equal \(U\): the sampler picks all of them.
  • Uniformly with equal minima and curvature; in general \(\propto 1/\sqrt{\det \nabla^2 U}\).
probability mass per basin

Fig. 2. \(e^{-U(x)/T}/Z(T)\) for the toy energy. As \(T\) decreases, the mass leaves the plateau and the shallow basins and concentrates on A and B. Below \(T^*\), the peaks are narrower than the plotting grid can resolve, so they are drawn as Dirac deltas with their probabilities.

1.3How to sample

The sampling problem can be solved with Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), or, as here, with Path Integral Sampling, which solves a special case of the Schrödinger bridge problem (from a single point to the target distribution, also known as the Schrödinger–Föllmer process). Before we head into it, note that sampling directly from the aforementioned Boltzmann distribution is very inefficient, given that the normalizing factor \(Z(T)\) requires an integration over all possible optimization variables, which is simply brute force. In this tutorial, we will focus on Path Integral Sampling as a means of optimization or, equivalently, of sampling from the constructed Boltzmann distribution.

  • Samplers: MCMC, or here, Path Integral Sampling.
  • Path Integral Sampling: a special case of the Schrödinger bridge (single point → target), the Schrödinger–Föllmer process.
  • Sampling the Boltzmann distribution directly is inefficient: \(Z(T)\) integrates over all variables, i.e., brute force.
  • Focus: Path Integral Sampling as optimization, i.e., sampling the Boltzmann distribution.

2Summary of the path integral sampler

2.1Stochastic optimal control

We start from the simplest possible drift-diffusion problem,

$$dX_t = u(t,X_t)\,dt + \sigma(t)\,dW_t, \tag{2}$$

with \(\sigma(t) > 0\) for all \(t \in [0,T]\). We would like to minimize

$$J(t,x) = \min_u \mathbb{E}_{Q^u}\left[\int_{t}^T \frac{\|u(s,X_s)\|^2}{2\sigma(s)^2}\,ds + \Psi(X_T)\,\middle|\, X_t = x\right]. \tag{3}$$

What we will come to later on is that, by choosing the right \(\Psi\), the solution to this Stochastic Optimal Control (SOC) problem will sample from any desired distribution (Eq. 11).

2.2HJB and Kolmogorov

It turns out that the solution to the problem arises from a PDE called the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation:

$$0 = \partial_t J - \frac{1}{2}\sigma(t)^2\,\partial_x J^\top \partial_x J + \frac{1}{2}\sigma(t)^2\, \mathrm{tr}\,\partial_{xx}J, \qquad J(T,x) = \Psi(x). \tag{4}$$
Derivation: Itô's lemma + Bellman's principle → HJB

Using Itô calculus (assuming \(J\) is \(C^{1,2}\)) with \(d[X,X]_t = \sigma(t)^2 I\,dt\):

$$J(t+dt, X_{t+dt}) = J(t,X_t) + \Big(\partial_t J + \partial_x J^\top u(t,X_t) + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma(t)^2\,\mathrm{tr}\,\partial_{xx}J\Big)dt + \sigma(t)\,\partial_x J^\top dW_t.$$

On the other hand, according to Bellman's equation (dynamic programming),

$$\begin{aligned} J(t,x) &= \min_{u} \mathbb{E}_{Q^u}\left[J(t+dt,X_{t+dt}) + \frac{\|u(t,X_t)\|^2}{2\sigma(t)^2}dt \,\middle|\, X_t = x\right]\\ &= \min_{u} \left[ J(t,x) + \Big(\partial_t J + \partial_x J^\top u(t,x) + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma(t)^2\,\mathrm{tr}\,\partial_{xx}J + \frac{\|u(t,x)\|^2}{2\sigma(t)^2}\Big)dt \right], \end{aligned}$$

where the \(dW_t\) term vanishes because \(\partial_x J(t,X_t)\) is \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-measurable and \(dW_t\) is independent of \(\mathcal{F}_t\) with zero mean. Subtracting \(J(t,x)\) and dividing by \(dt\):

$$0 = \min_{u} \Big(\partial_t J + \partial_x J^\top u + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma(t)^2\,\mathrm{tr}\,\partial_{xx}J + \frac{\|u\|^2}{2\sigma(t)^2}\Big).$$

The term is quadratic in \(u\); setting its gradient to zero, \(\partial_x J + u/\sigma(t)^2 = 0\), gives \(u^* = -\sigma(t)^2\partial_x J\), and substituting it back gives Eq. 4.

This shows that a smooth value function satisfies the HJB equation. The converse (a smooth solution of HJB is the value function and \(u^*\) is optimal) needs a separate verification argument, which we omit.

By introducing \(\phi(t,x) = e^{-J(t,x)}\) (the Cole–Hopf transform), we arrive at the linear Kolmogorov backward equation:

$$\partial_t \phi + \frac{1}{2}\sigma(t)^2\,\mathrm{tr}\,\partial_{xx}\phi = 0, \qquad \phi(T,x) = e^{-\Psi(x)}. \tag{5}$$
Derivation: Cole–Hopf (log) transform
$$\begin{aligned} \partial_x J &= -\frac{\partial_x \phi}{\phi}, & \partial_x J^\top \partial_x J &= \frac{\|\partial_x \phi\|^2}{\phi^2}, & \partial_t J &= -\frac{\partial_t\phi}{\phi}, & \mathrm{tr}\,\partial_{xx}J &= \frac{-\phi\, \mathrm{tr}\,\partial_{xx}\phi + \|\partial_x \phi\|^2}{\phi^2}. \end{aligned}$$

Substituting into Eq. 4, the \(\|\partial_x\phi\|^2\) terms cancel:

$$-\frac{\partial_t \phi}{\phi} - \sigma(t)^2\frac{\|\partial_x \phi\|^2}{2\phi^2} + \sigma(t)^2\frac{-\phi\, \mathrm{tr}\,\partial_{xx}\phi + \|\partial_x \phi\|^2}{2\phi^2} = -\frac{\partial_t \phi}{\phi} - \sigma(t)^2\frac{\mathrm{tr}\,\partial_{xx}\phi}{2\phi} = 0,$$

and multiplying by \(-\phi\) gives Eq. 5. The optimal control becomes

$$u^* = -\sigma(t)^2\partial_x J = \sigma(t)^2\,\partial_x \log \phi.$$

2.3The optimal control

It further turns out that the solution to our SOC problem (Eq. 3) comes from

$$u^*(t,x) = \sigma(t)^2\,\partial_x\log \phi = \sigma(t)^2\,\frac{\mathbb{E}_{Q^0}\left[e^{-\Psi(X_T)}\int_t^s \sigma(r)\,dW_r \,\middle|\, X_t=x\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{Q^0}\left[e^{-\Psi(X_T)} \,\middle|\, X_t=x\right]\int_t^s \sigma(r)^2\, dr} \quad \forall s \in (t,T], \tag{6}$$

where the expectation in the denominator is \(\phi\) itself, given by the Feynman–Kac representation of the solution of Eq. 5,

$$\phi(t,x) = \mathbb{E}_{Q^0}\left[e^{-\Psi(X_T)} \,\middle|\, X_t = x\right]. \tag{7}$$
Derivation: Feynman–Kac (Eq. 7)

If \(u=0\) (i.e., \(dX_t = \sigma(t)\,dW_t\)), under the measure \(Q^0\), Itô's lemma and Eq. 5 give

$$d\phi = \underbrace{\Big(\partial_t \phi + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma(t)^2\,\mathrm{tr}\,\partial_{xx}\phi\Big)}_{0}dt + \partial_x \phi^\top dX_t.$$

Integrating over \([t,T]\) and taking the expectation (the stochastic integral has zero mean when it is a true martingale):

$$\mathbb{E}_{Q^0}\left[\phi(T,X_T) - \phi(t,X_t)\,\middle|\, X_t = x\right] = 0 \implies \phi(t,x) = \mathbb{E}_{Q^0}\left[e^{-\Psi(X_T)}\,\middle|\, X_t = x\right].$$
Derivation: two ways to compute \(\partial_x\phi\)

Under \(Q^0\), \(X_T = x + Z\) with \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}\big(0, (\int_t^T \sigma(r)^2 dr)\, I\big)\).

  1. If \(\Psi\) is differentiable:

    $$\partial_x \mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[e^{-\Psi(x + Z)}\right] = -\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\partial_x \Psi(x+Z)\, e^{-\Psi(x + Z)}\right].$$

  2. Without needing \(\Psi\) to be differentiable, write the expectation as an integral against the Gaussian kernel \(p(z) \propto e^{-\|z\|^2 / 2\int_t^T \sigma(r)^2 dr}\) and differentiate the kernel:

    $$\partial_x \int e^{-\Psi(y)} p(y-x)\, dy = \int e^{-\Psi(y)}\frac{y-x}{\int_t^T \sigma(r)^2 dr}\, p(y-x)\, dy = \frac{\mathbb{E}_{Q^0}\left[e^{-\Psi(X_T)} \int_t^T \sigma(r)\,dW_r \,\middle|\, X_t = x\right]}{\int_t^T \sigma(r)^2 dr}.$$

In both cases \(\partial_x\) is exchanged with the expectation (dominated convergence, e.g., \(\Psi\) bounded below).

Derivation: why any intermediate time \(s \in (t,T]\) works

Integrating \(d\phi\) over \([t,s]\) instead gives \(\phi(t,x) = \mathbb{E}_{Q^0}[\phi(s,X_s)\,|\,X_t = x]\), and the same kernel trick yields

$$\partial_x \phi(t,x) = \frac{\mathbb{E}_{Q^0}\left[\phi(s,X_s) \int_t^s \sigma(r)\,dW_r \,\middle|\, X_t\right]}{\int_t^s \sigma(r)^2 dr}.$$

Since \(\phi(s,X_s) = \mathbb{E}_{Q^0}[e^{-\Psi(X_T)}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_s]\),

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_{Q^0}\left[\phi(s,X_s) \int_t^s \sigma\,dW \,\middle|\, X_t\right] &= \mathbb{E}_{Q^0}\left[\mathbb{E}_{Q^0}\left[e^{-\Psi(X_T)}\,\middle|\, \mathcal{F}_s\right] \int_t^s \sigma\,dW \,\middle|\, \mathcal{F}_t\right]\\ &= \mathbb{E}_{Q^0}\left[\mathbb{E}_{Q^0}\left[e^{-\Psi(X_T)}\int_t^s \sigma\,dW \,\middle|\, \mathcal{F}_s\right] \,\middle|\, \mathcal{F}_t\right] = \mathbb{E}_{Q^0}\left[e^{-\Psi(X_T)}\int_t^s \sigma\,dW \,\middle|\, X_t\right]. \end{aligned}$$

The steps use: the Markov property (conditioning on \(\mathcal{F}_t\) is the same as on \(X_t\)); \(\int_t^s \sigma\,dW\) is \(\mathcal{F}_s\)-measurable (pull-out property); and the tower property, since \(\mathcal{F}_t \subset \mathcal{F}_s\). Dividing by \(\phi\) gives Eq. 6.

2.4Back to square one?

Please note that the expectations in Eq. 6 are calculated under the measure \(Q^0\), which means that \(X_T\) is distributed as under pure noise. Now the problem is that evaluating the optimal control this way is highly inefficient; indeed, it is about as inefficient as sampling directly from the Boltzmann distribution, so it feels like we are back to square one.

  • Eq. 6 is under \(Q^0\): \(X_T\) is pure noise.
  • Evaluating \(u^*\) this way is as inefficient as sampling the Boltzmann distribution directly.
  • Back to square one.

2.5Learning the control

However, here we can do a trick that is less natural for MCMC and PSO. The expectations in Eqs. 6–7 can equally be taken under any controlled measure \(Q^u\) and reweighted (importance sampling with Girsanov's theorem), so we can learn \(u_\theta\), for instance as the output of a Neural Network (NN), which gradually becomes better and hopefully gets close to the actual \(u^*\): each improvement of \(u_\theta\) makes the next batch of paths, and therefore the next estimate, better (Fig. 3). Unlike a Markov chain or a swarm, the result is a reusable sampler: a single SDE rollout gives a new independent sample, and the network can be conditioned on the problem, so the search does not restart from scratch for every new problem.

  • Trick: take the expectations in Eqs. 6–7 under any controlled \(Q^u\) and reweight (importance sampling, Girsanov).
  • Learn \(u_\theta\) (e.g., a NN) that gradually gets close to \(u^*\).
  • Better \(u_\theta\) → better paths → better estimate (Fig. 3).
  • Unlike MCMC/PSO: a reusable sampler; one SDE rollout = one new independent sample.
  • Can be conditioned on the problem: no restart from scratch.
Importance sampling under \(Q^u\) (Girsanov)
$$\left(\frac{dQ^0}{dQ^u}\right)_{[t,T]} = \exp\left(-\int_t^T\frac{u^\top dX_s}{\sigma(s)^2} + \frac12\int_t^T\frac{\|u\|^2}{\sigma(s)^2}\,ds\right),$$ $$\begin{aligned}\phi(t,x) &= \mathbb{E}_{Q^u}\left[e^{-\Psi(X_T)}\left(\frac{dQ^0}{dQ^u}\right)_{[t,T]}\,\middle|\, X_t = x\right]\\ &= \mathbb{E}_{Q^u}\left[\exp\left(-\Psi(X_T) - \int_t^T\frac{u^\top dX_s}{\sigma(s)^2} + \frac12\int_t^T\frac{\|u\|^2}{\sigma(s)^2}\,ds\right)\middle|\, X_t = x\right].\end{aligned}$$

The closer \(u\) is to \(u^*\), the less the weights vary between paths; at \(u = u^*\) every path has the same weight.

To train such a controller, we can use the forward KL divergence \(D_{KL}(Q^*\,\|\,Q^{u_\theta})\) between the optimal path measure and the one the network drives (Path Integral Cross-Entropy, PICE).

2.6Forward KL (PICE)

The gradient of the forward KL cannot be calculated directly, since its expectation is under \(Q^*\), which we cannot sample from, so we need to resort to importance sampling. More concretely, we need to evaluate

  • Expectation under \(Q^*\), which we cannot sample → importance sampling.
  • We need:
$$-\partial_{\theta}D_{KL}\left(Q^* \,\middle\|\, Q^{u_\theta} \,\middle|\, X_t = x\right)_{[t,T]} = \mathbb{E}_{Q^*}\left[\int_t^T\frac{\partial_{\theta}u_{\theta}(s,X_s)^\top dX_s}{\sigma(s)^2} - \int_t^T\frac{\partial_{\theta}u_{\theta}^\top u_{\theta}}{\sigma(s)^2}\,ds\,\middle|\, X_t = x\right]. \tag{8}$$
Derivation: Eq. 8
$$\begin{aligned} D_{KL}\left(Q^* \,\middle\|\, Q^{u_\theta} \,\middle|\, X_t = x\right)_{[t,T]} &= \mathbb{E}_{Q^*}\left[\log\left(\frac{dQ^*}{dQ^{u_\theta}}\right)_{[t,T]}\middle|\, X_t = x\right]\\ &= \mathbb{E}_{Q^*}\left[\log \left(\frac{dQ^0}{dQ^{u_\theta}}\right)_{[t,T]}\middle|\, X_t = x\right] + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{Q^*}\left[\log \left(\frac{dQ^*}{dQ^{0}}\right)_{[t,T]}\middle|\, X_t = x\right]}_{\text{const in } \theta}\\ &= \mathbb{E}_{Q^*}\left[-\int_t^T\frac{u_\theta^\top dX_s}{\sigma(s)^2} + \frac12\int_t^T\frac{\|u_\theta\|^2}{\sigma(s)^2}\,ds\,\middle|\, X_t = x\right] + \text{const}. \end{aligned}$$

\(Q^*\) does not depend on \(\theta\), so differentiating under the expectation gives Eq. 8.

The expectation is under \(Q^*\). The density of the optimal path measure \(Q^* = Q^{u^*}\) with respect to the uncontrolled one is

$$\left(\frac{dQ^*}{dQ^{0}}\right)_{[t,T]} = \frac{e^{-\Psi(X_T)}}{\phi(t,x)}. \tag{9}$$
Derivation: Eq. 9

Eq. 9 follows from Girsanov with \(u^*\) in place of \(u\):

$$\begin{aligned} \left(\frac{dQ^*}{dQ^{0}}\right)_{[t,T]} &= \exp\left(\int_t^T\frac{u^{*\top} dX_s}{\sigma(s)^2} -\frac12\int_t^T\frac{\|u^*\|^2}{\sigma(s)^2}\,ds \right)\\ &= \exp\left(\int_t^T \partial_x\log\phi^\top dX_s -\frac12\int_t^T\sigma(s)^2\|\partial_x\log\phi\|^2\,ds \right)\\ &= \exp\left(\int_t^T d\log \phi \right)\\ &= \frac{\phi(T,X_T)}{\phi(t,x)}\\ &= \frac{e^{-\Psi(X_T)}}{\phi(t,x)}. \end{aligned}$$

The first step is Girsanov, the second uses \(u^* = \sigma^2\partial_x\log\phi\), and the integrands equal \(d\log\phi\) because, by Itô and Eq. 5,

$$\begin{aligned} d\log\phi &= \underbrace{\Big(\frac{\partial_t\phi}{\phi} + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\frac{\mathrm{tr}\,\partial_{xx}\phi}{\phi}\Big)}_{0}ds\\ &\quad - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\|\partial_x\log\phi\|^2\,ds + \partial_x\log\phi^\top dX_s. \end{aligned}$$

By importance sampling, with Eq. 9, we end up with

$$\mathbb{E}_{Q^*}\left[F\,\middle|\, X_t = x\right] = \frac{1}{\phi(t,x)}\,\mathbb{E}_{Q^{u_{\theta}}}\left[F \exp\left(-\int_t^T\frac{u_{\theta}^\top dX_s}{\sigma(s)^2} + \frac12\int_t^T\frac{\|u_{\theta}\|^2}{\sigma(s)^2}\,ds -\Psi(X_T)\right)\middle|\, X_t = x\right],$$

where \(F \coloneqq \int_t^T\frac{\partial_{\theta}u_{\theta}(s,X_s)^\top dX_s}{\sigma(s)^2} - \int_t^T\frac{\partial_{\theta}u_{\theta}^\top u_{\theta}}{\sigma(s)^2}\,ds\) is the gradient term. In the algorithm, we specifically use the self-normalized weights

$$S^{(i)} = \int_t^T\frac{u_{\theta}^\top dX^{(i)}_s}{\sigma(s)^2} - \frac12\int_t^T\frac{\|u_{\theta}\|^2}{\sigma(s)^2}\,ds +\Psi(X^{(i)}_T), \qquad \tilde{w}_i = e^{-S^{(i)}}, \qquad w_i = \frac{\tilde{w}_i}{\sum_{j=1}^N \tilde{w}_j} \tag{10}$$

to update the network, with \(\sum_i w_i F^{(i)}\) as the (ascent) gradient.

Derivation: importance sampling and self-normalization
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_{Q^*}\left[F\,\middle|\, X_t = x\right] &= \mathbb{E}_{Q^{u_\theta}}\left[F \left(\frac{dQ^*}{dQ^{0}}\right)_{[t,T]}\left(\frac{dQ^0}{dQ^{u_\theta}}\right)_{[t,T]}\middle|\, X_t = x\right] = \mathbb{E}_{Q^{u_\theta}}\left[F \,\frac{e^{-\Psi(X_T)}}{\phi(t,x)}\left(\frac{dQ^0}{dQ^{u_\theta}}\right)_{[t,T]}\middle|\, X_t = x\right], \end{aligned}$$

using \((dQ^*/dQ^0)_{[t,T]} = e^{-\Psi(X_T)}/\phi(t,x)\) (Eq. 9) and Girsanov, which gives the expression above. The unknown \(\phi(t,x)\) is itself an expectation under \(Q^{u_\theta}\), \(\phi(t,x) = \mathbb{E}_{Q^{u_\theta}}[e^{-S}]\) (Section 2.5), so estimating both with the same \(N\) paths gives \(\mathbb{E}_{Q^*}[F] \approx \sum_i w_i F^{(i)}\), i.e., the self-normalized importance sampling (SNIS) of Eq. 10.

In the code (PI.py), \(\sum_i w_i F^{(i)}\) is computed as the gradient of a weighted regression of the drift on the path increments, \(\tfrac12\sum_i w_i \sum_k \frac{\Delta t}{\sigma_k^2}\big\|\Delta X^{(i)}_k/\Delta t - u_\theta(t_k, X^{(i)}_k)\big\|^2\), whose negative \(\theta\)-gradient is the discretized \(\sum_i w_i F^{(i)}\).

2.7The Schrödinger–Föllmer sampler

So far, we have talked about how to solve a general SOC problem, but we haven't talked about the Schrödinger–Föllmer part. If we set \(\Psi = \log(d\mu^0/d\mu)\), where \(\mu^0\) is the Gaussian distribution constructed by pure Brownian motion (the endpoint distribution of the uncontrolled SDE started at \(x_0\)) and \(\mu\) is the target distribution, the optimally controlled SDE is proved to be a sampler of the target distribution:

  • So far: a general SOC problem. Missing: the Schrödinger–Föllmer part.
  • \(\Psi = \log(d\mu^0/d\mu)\); \(\mu^0\): the Gaussian from pure Brownian motion, \(\mu\): the target.
  • Then the optimally controlled SDE is proved to sample \(\mu\):
$$\Psi(x) = \log\frac{d\mu^0}{d\mu}(x) = \log\mu^0(x) - \log\mu(x) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X^{u^*}_T \sim \mu, \tag{11}$$

where \(\mu^0 = \mathcal{N}(x_0, \bar\sigma^2 I)\) with \(\bar\sigma^2 = \int_0^T \sigma(r)^2\,dr\). The normalizing constant of \(\mu\) is not needed: it shifts \(\Psi\) by a constant, which scales \(\phi\) but leaves \(u^* = \sigma^2\partial_x\log\phi\) unchanged.

Proof (Zhang & Chen, 2022, Theorem 1)

Lemma (Zhang & Chen, 2022, Lemma 4.1). The transition density associated with the optimal control \(u^*\) of Eqs. 2–3 is

$$Q^*_{s,t}(x,y) = Q^0_{s,t}(x,y)\,\frac{\phi(t,y)}{\phi(s,x)},$$

where \(Q^u_{s,t}(x,y)\) denotes the transition density from state \(x\) at time \(s\) to state \(y\) at time \(t\).

Why: the lemma from Eq. 9

Restricting Eq. 9 (on \([s,T]\), started at \(X_s = x\)) to the paths up to time \(t\) means conditioning the density on \(\mathcal{F}_t\):

$$\left(\frac{dQ^*}{dQ^0}\right)_{[s,t]} = \mathbb{E}_{Q^0}\left[\frac{e^{-\Psi(X_T)}}{\phi(s,x)}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_t\right] = \frac{\phi(t,X_t)}{\phi(s,x)},$$

by the Markov property and Eq. 7. The density depends on the path only through \(X_t = y\), which gives the lemma.

Proof of the theorem. We denote the initial Dirac distribution by \(\nu = \delta_{x_0}\). Combining Eq. 7 and \(J(t,x) = -\log\phi(t,x)\), and using that under \(Q^0\) the endpoint is \(X_T \sim \mu^0\) and \(e^{-\Psi} = d\mu/d\mu^0\), we obtain

$$J(0,x_0) = -\log\mathbb{E}_{Q^0}\left[e^{-\Psi(X_T)}\right] = -\log\left(\int \frac{d\mu}{d\mu^0}\,d\mu^0\right) = -\log 1 = 0.$$

Therefore, we can evaluate the KL divergence between \(Q^*\) and \(Q^0(\tau\,|\,x_T)\,\mu(x_T)\) (Brownian paths pinned at an endpoint drawn from \(\mu\)) as

$$D_{KL}\Big(Q^*(\tau)\,\Big\|\,Q^0(\tau\,|\,x_T)\,\mu(x_T)\Big) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim Q^*}\left[\int_0^T \frac{\|u^*_t\|^2}{2\sigma(t)^2}\,dt + \Psi(x_T)\right] = J(0,x_0) = 0.$$
The two equalities

Since \(Q^0(\tau) = Q^0(\tau\,|\,x_T)\,\mu^0(x_T)\),

$$\log\frac{dQ^*}{d\big[Q^0(\cdot\,|\,x_T)\,\mu\big]}(\tau) = \log\frac{dQ^*}{dQ^0}(\tau) + \log\frac{d\mu^0}{d\mu}(x_T) = \log\frac{dQ^*}{dQ^0}(\tau) + \Psi(x_T),$$

and by Girsanov, since under \(Q^*\) we have \(dX_t = u^*_t\,dt + \sigma(t)\,dW_t\),

$$\log\frac{dQ^*}{dQ^0}(\tau) = \int_0^T\frac{u_t^{*\top} dX_t}{\sigma(t)^2} - \frac12\int_0^T\frac{\|u^*_t\|^2}{\sigma(t)^2}\,dt = \int_0^T\frac{\|u^*_t\|^2}{2\sigma(t)^2}\,dt + \underbrace{\int_0^T\frac{u_t^{*\top} dW_t}{\sigma(t)}}_{\text{zero mean}},$$

so \(\mathbb{E}_{Q^*}\big[\log\frac{dQ^*}{dQ^0}\big] = \mathbb{E}_{Q^*}\big[\int_0^T \frac{\|u^*_t\|^2}{2\sigma(t)^2}dt\big]\). This is the first equality. The second one holds because \(u^*\) is the optimal control, so its expected cost (Eq. 3) is the value \(J(0,x_0)\).

Next, we show that \(x^*_T \sim \mu\). The above equations (or the lemma with \(s = 0\), \(t = T\), \(\phi(T,y) = e^{-\Psi(y)}\) and \(\phi(0,x_0) = e^{-J(0,x_0)} = 1\)) imply \(Q^*_{0,T}(x_0,y)\,dy = e^{-\Psi(y)}\,\mu^0(dy)\). It follows that

$$\mathbb{P}\left[x^*_T \in A\right] = \int_A Q^*_{0,T}(x_0,y)\,dy = \int_A e^{-\Psi(y)}\,\mu^0(dy) = \mu(A).$$

With an unnormalized \(\mu\) (normalizing constant \(Z\)), \(\phi(0,x_0) = Z\) instead of 1, and the same steps give \(x^*_T \sim \mu/Z\).

3PICE on the toy problem

3.1Setup

We now train PICE on the toy problem of Section 1. The target is the Boltzmann distribution of \(U\) at the temperature \(T\), and with the SDE starting at \(x_0 = 0\), Eq. 11 gives the terminal cost

  • PICE on the toy of Section 1; target: the Boltzmann distribution of \(U\) at temperature \(T\).
  • SDE starts at \(x_0 = 0\); terminal cost from Eq. 11:
$$\mu(x) \propto e^{-U(x)/T}, \qquad \Psi(x) = \frac{U(x)}{T} - \frac{\|x\|^2}{2\bar\sigma^2}. \tag{12}$$

At \(T = 0.05\), 99.5% of the mass of \(\mu\) is on A and B, split evenly. So the sampler has to find both narrow basins, far from each other and from the start, while E, the basin next to the start, holds almost none of it.

  • At \(T = 0.05\): 99.5% of \(\mu\) on A and B, split evenly.
  • The sampler must find both narrow, distant basins; E, next to the start, holds almost none of the mass.
Toy settings

Here the SDE horizon is \([0,1]\), and \(T\) denotes the temperature. \(T = 0.05\), 100 Euler–Maruyama steps, \(\sigma(t)\) linear from __SIGMA_MAX__ to 0.05 so that \(\bar\sigma = 3.5\), 512 paths per iteration, an MLP drift (3 × 128, SiLU) initialized to \(u = 0\), one Adam step per iteration (learning rate \(10^{-3}\)), 2000 iterations.

Basins (minimum energy, bowl width): A and B 0.00 (0.45); C 0.30, D 0.60, E 0.90, F 1.20 (0.9 each).

3.2Training demo

To see the training in effect, we can look at the demo below. At first, only a handful of paths land inside the narrow basins A and B, so one or two paths carry almost all of the weight (effective sample size at most 10 of 512 for __ESS_LOW__ of the first __ESS_WINDOW__ iterations), and the update swings between A and B. Still, those few paths guide the model in the right direction, as they do in a well-behaved problem with enough exploration and wide enough basins: from about iteration __RESOLVED_IT__ on, fewer than 10% of the paths are left unresolved, and at iteration __LAST_IT__, __FRAC_A__ of the paths are in A, __FRAC_B__ in B, and __UNRES__ unresolved.
